CRGO vs amorphous cores: a buyer's guide

Updated 19 June 2026 · ~5 min read · TransformerPath Knowledge

The core is where a transformer spends energy doing nothing — its no-load loss runs 24/7 for 30+ years. Two materials dominate the choice: conventional grain-oriented silicon steel (CRGO) and amorphous metal. The right pick depends on loss economics, application and supply, not on one being universally "better."

How they differ

CRGO is a crystalline silicon steel rolled so its magnetic grains align with the flux — decades of refinement (high-permeability, laser/plasma domain-refined grades) have driven its losses steadily down. Amorphous metal is a glassy iron-boron-silicon ribbon, frozen so fast it has no crystal structure at all, which slashes hysteresis loss.

FactorCRGO silicon steelAmorphous metal
No-load (core) lossLowVery low — typically ~60–70% lower
Load (copper) lossComparableComparable (winding-driven)
Core size / weightCompactLarger & heavier for same rating
NoiseLowerHigher — needs design attention
Saturation flux densityHigher (~1.9 T)Lower (~1.5 T)
Cost / unitLower upfrontHigher upfront
ManufacturingMature, widely tooledSpecialist; brittle ribbon, fewer makers

When amorphous wins

Amorphous excels wherever no-load loss is the dominant cost — distribution transformers that sit lightly loaded for most of their life, on grids where energy is expensive or carbon is priced. Over a long service life the energy saved can outweigh the higher purchase price and bigger footprint. It's why several countries with strong efficiency mandates pushed amorphous distribution transformers hard.

When CRGO wins

CRGO remains the default for power transformers and most heavily-loaded units, where load loss and size matter more, higher flux density keeps the core compact, and lower noise is valuable. It's also the pragmatic choice when capital cost, delivery and a deep, mature supply base are decisive — and modern high-permeability grades have narrowed the loss gap considerably.

The buyer's decision

  • Run the total-owning-cost numbers. Capitalise no-load and load loss over the expected life at the local energy price. If no-load loss capitalisation is high and the unit is lightly loaded, amorphous often pays back.
  • Check the loading profile. Lightly loaded, always-energised distribution → amorphous-friendly. Heavily loaded power units → CRGO-friendly.
  • Mind size, weight and noise limits. Tight footprints, weight-limited pads or noise-sensitive sites favour CRGO.
  • Confirm supply. Amorphous has fewer ribbon suppliers and core builders; CRGO is broadly available but premium grades are tight. Either way, secure material allocation early (see our lead-times guide).

There's no single answer — the best core is the one that minimises total owning cost for that specific duty, on that grid, within the size, noise and delivery constraints of the project.
