Standard Transformer Oil Tests

Published 27 Aug 2026Updated 20 Oct 20268 min readIntermediateDiagnostics
Quick answer — A routine insulating-oil set measures dissolved gases, breakdown voltage, moisture, acidity, interfacial tension, furans and the physical checks: flash point, density, viscosity, colour, sludge and pour point. Gases point to electrical or thermal stress, a low breakdown voltage points to moisture or particles, and the physical tests show whether the fluid still matches its grade. A dissolved-gas sample is typically a 50 cc syringe. The other tests typically use a 1–1.5 L bottle. A routine set is often reported in 2–3 days.

What do the standard transformer oil tests show?

Sampling and turnaround

A dissolved-gas sample is typically drawn into a 50 cc glass syringe so the oil stays sealed from air. Breakdown voltage, moisture, acidity and the physical checks are typically taken in a 1–1.5 L bottle. Laboratories often report a routine set in 2–3 days. That interval is typical practice. A particular laboratory's quoted time is the one on its own offer.

Dissolved-gas analysis (ASTM D3612)

ASTM D3612 measures the gases dissolved in the oil by gas chromatography: commonly hydrogen, methane, ethane, ethylene, acetylene, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, and often oxygen and nitrogen. Heat and electrical discharge generate those gases in the oil and the paper. In general, hydrogen is associated with partial discharge and low-temperature faults, methane and ethane with lower-temperature overheating, ethylene with hotter thermal faults, acetylene with arcing, and the carbon oxides with the cellulose insulation. One sample is read against the same transformer's earlier samples. IEC 60599 and IEEE C57.104 set out how to classify the pattern. ASTM D3612 is the measurement method.

Breakdown voltage (IEC 60156)

IEC 60156 raises the voltage across a small oil-filled gap until the oil breaks down. The result is the dielectric strength of that sample. A low breakdown voltage is read as moisture, particles or other contamination in the oil. A high breakdown voltage means the sample withstood a higher voltage in the test cell. The number describes the oil sample. The transformer's rated insulation level is a separate rating.

Moisture (IEC 60814)

IEC 60814 measures water in the oil by coulometric Karl Fischer titration, usually in mg/kg. Water lowers the breakdown voltage, and it moves between the oil and the paper as the temperature changes. The same concentration corresponds to wetter paper when the oil is cooler, because water leaves the oil and enters the cellulose. A rising trend, read at a known oil temperature, is the signal that the insulation is taking on moisture.

Acid number (IEC 62021)

IEC 62021 measures the neutralization number, the acidity of the oil, commonly in mg KOH/g. Acids form as the oil oxidizes. A rising acid number means oxidation is progressing. At higher acidity the oil is more likely to form sludge, and the acids can attack the solid insulation. The result is compared with the supervision guidance for the fluid in service, IEC 60422 for mineral oil, and with that transformer's own history.

Interfacial tension (ASTM D971)

ASTM D971 measures the interfacial tension of the oil against water by the ring method, in mN/m. Clean, lightly oxidized oil holds a high interfacial tension. As polar oxidation products and contaminants build up, the tension falls. A falling value is read with acidity and colour as a sign that the oil is aging.

Furans (ASTM D5837)

ASTM D5837 measures furanic compounds in the oil, including 2-furaldehyde, which the cellulose releases as it degrades. Rising furans indicate that the paper is aging, or that a local hot spot is damaging it. The result complements dissolved-gas analysis: the gases describe stress in the oil and at a fault, and the furans describe the solid insulation. A furan result is a condition indicator. Any estimate of remaining paper life uses a chosen calculation and the history of that insulation.

Flash point

Flash point is the lowest temperature at which vapour from the oil ignites under the test conditions. Laboratories commonly use a closed-cup method (ASTM D93 or ISO 2719) or an open-cup method (ASTM D92). A result in line with the fluid specification means the oil has kept its volatility. A result below that specification is read as contamination by a more volatile liquid, such as a fuel or a solvent, or as severe degradation.

Density

Density, and relative density, identify the fluid and convert a volume to a mass. It is commonly measured with a digital density meter (ASTM D4052 or ISO 12185) or a hydrometer (ASTM D1298 or ISO 3675). The result is compared with the specification of the oil that was filled. A shift from that specification is read as a mixture with a different fluid.

Viscosity

Kinematic viscosity, commonly reported at 40°C by ASTM D445 or ISO 3104, is how readily the oil flows. Cooling depends on that flow. The result is compared with the grade of the fluid in the tank. A viscosity above that grade slows circulation and the removal of heat.

Colour

Colour is reported on the ASTM colour scale, ASTM D1500, also published as ISO 2049. New mineral oil is light. Darkening accompanies oxidation, contamination and material dissolved from the insulation. Colour supports the other results. Dielectric condition is read from breakdown voltage, moisture and the dissolved-gas tests.

Sludge

Sludge is insoluble material thrown out as the oil oxidizes. Oxidation-stability tests such as IEC 61125 report sludge as a share of the sample, and a used oil may be inspected for sediment. Visible sludge, or a rising sludge result, means oxidation has produced solids. Those solids can settle in ducts and on windings and restrict cooling.

Pour point

Pour point, ASTM D97 or ISO 3016, is the lowest temperature at which the oil still flows under the test conditions. Cold-weather circulation depends on a pour point below the lowest oil temperature the transformer will see. Oil that reaches its pour point in service can stop flowing, and it then stops carrying heat away from the windings.

Reading the set together

Dissolved gas and furans describe faults and paper aging. Breakdown voltage and moisture describe dielectric condition. Acidity, interfacial tension, colour and sludge describe oxidation. Flash point, density, viscosity and pour point describe whether the fluid is still the grade that was filled. Action limits sit in the supervision guide for that fluid and in the trend for that transformer.
